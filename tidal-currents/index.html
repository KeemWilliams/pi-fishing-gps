Tide and current app features: NOAA predicted current arrows

The map draws NOAA's predicted tidal current as an arrow at each current station, with a speed and a flood or ebb marker. Every one of them says Predicted, because none of it is measured at the boat.

Built Works on demo values. · Last changed 2026-10-28

What the arrows show

Moving water decides where a lot of fish sit, so current is the first thing I want to see on the map. The Current layer draws one arrow for each NOAA current prediction station in view. The arrow points the way the water moves. An arrow with a white body is flood, an arrow in rust is ebb, and the legend on screen says which is which, so colour is not the only cue. A tag next to each arrow gives the speed, and a station near slack water says "Slack".

The arrows move slowly in the direction of flow. The animation is there to show direction at a glance.

The panel under the map says which time the arrows are for ("Current for 11:40 PM"), and tapping an arrow opens a card for that station with the next slack, flood and ebb times.

Predicted, not measured

The numbers come from NOAA CO-OPS tidal current predictions. They are harmonic predictions made for a station position, not readings from a meter at the boat. Three consequences:

  • Wind, river flow after rain and storm surge can change real current by a lot, and a prediction does not include them.
  • One arrow per station is not a flow field. Between stations I do not interpolate, so I do not draw anything there.
  • A speed below about 0.15 knots is treated as slack for fishing purposes.

The Today screen has a Tide card in the same spirit. It shows whether the tide is rising or falling and the next predicted high and low, with a Predicted tag. The screenshots show demo values and say so on the screen.

Where the data comes from

Predictions and tide times come from the NOAA Center for Operational Oceanographic Products and Services (CO-OPS) public data API. The Pi fetches them when it has a connection and stores them, so the arrows and tide times keep working offline for as long as the stored predictions cover. Because they are predictions, they can be fetched days ahead. Speeds are shown in miles per hour, with knots as a setting.

CO-OPS is US government data, and the data sources page credits it. For the research behind the bite score I also used CO-OPS current meter records from seven stations, but that is a different use and it is described on the bite score page.

What I have not done

  • I have not compared the predicted arrows with what a real boat feels on the water.
  • A real Pi has not fetched live CO-OPS data yet, because I do not have one.
  • Current at a specific spot between stations is not estimated at all.
